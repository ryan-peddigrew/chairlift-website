Neon accents Confirmed

Neon ties the page to the logo, used in a few small places only. Everything else is Midnight Ink on light backgrounds and white on Ink.

Where Neon is used

  • The top step of the logo icon (website, favicon and email)
  • The up-right arrow after "Make the climb easier"
  • The short bar above the hero headline
  • The short bars above titles on Ink sections
  • The "After" label on Ink
  • The ring around the founder photo on Who we are (matches the email signature)
  • Email signatures only: the Ink signature's headshot ring, divider line, "FOUNDER" label

Where Neon is not used on the website

  • Text, key words, highlights or underlines
  • Check marks, buttons or tags
  • Bars above titles on white or grey sections (these stay Ink)
  • The heading "Where we usually start." (never a line, bar, underline or highlight)

Website layout Confirmed

Decisions made while building usechairlift.com.

Do

  • Straight, full-width lines between sections
  • Alternate White, Mist and Ink sections so two Ink sections never touch
  • White cards on Ink sections; the homepage "What we take off your team's plate" cards are the one exception (first card white, the other two lighter Ink with a white top bar)
  • A short solid bar above each section title
  • Section titles in ALL CAPS ending with a full stop
  • "Sound familiar?" statements in prominent ALL CAPS

Don't

  • Stepped or staircase edges between sections
  • Staircase illustrations, dotted lines or square decorations anywhere outside the logo. Never.
  • Indented headlines
  • Italic words inside titles (except the light italic "On" in Onboard)
  • Half-Neon title bars

Voice Confirmed

Professional, but friendly and easy to read. Write like a capable person talking to a busy owner.

Do

  • Say the benefit plainly and early (time back, lower costs)
  • Use "we" on the website; "I" only in the founder's own note
  • Keep sentences short and specific
  • Cite real, linkable sources for any industry claim
  • Let the reader decide what fits their business

Don't

  • Assume a prospect's size, budget or competitors
  • Invent clients, results, statistics or testimonials
  • Call illustrative examples "case studies"
  • Use jargon or phrases like "big IT project"
  • Use "quietly" or "one process at a time" as headline copy
