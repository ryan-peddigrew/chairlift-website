Neon accents Confirmed

Neon is a complement, never the main event. It doesn't read well as text on white, so on light backgrounds it sits behind words; on Ink it can be the text colour.

Key word · light background

Where we usually start.

Neon highlighter behind one word per title.

Key word · Ink background

Journey to the top.

Neon text, one word per title.

Where Neon is used

  • The up arrow after "Make the climb easier"
  • One key word in a title (run itself, start, top, practice, people, hours)
  • The check mark next to "Built inside the tools you already use"
  • Link underlines
  • Small numbers and labels on Ink (step numbers, "After")
  • Email signatures: the headshot ring in both versions; on the Ink version also the name and the divider line; the logo's top step
  • The top step of the logo

Where Neon is not used

  • The short bars above titles (these are solid Ink, or white on Ink)
  • Body text, buttons or large areas
  • As text colour on white backgrounds
  • More than one accent word per title

Website layout Confirmed

Decisions made while building usechairlift.com.

Do

  • Straight, full-width lines between sections
  • Alternate White, Mist and Ink sections so two Ink sections never touch
  • White cards on Ink sections (no dark-on-dark cards)
  • A short solid bar above each section title
  • Section titles in ALL CAPS ending with a full stop
  • "Sound familiar?" statements in prominent ALL CAPS

Don't

  • Stepped or staircase edges between sections
  • Staircase illustrations in headers or boxes
  • Indented headlines
  • Italic words inside titles
  • Half-Neon title bars

Voice Confirmed

Professional, but friendly and easy to read. Write like a capable person talking to a busy owner.

Do

  • Say the benefit plainly and early (time back, lower costs)
  • Use "we" on the website; "I" only in the founder's own note
  • Keep sentences short and specific
  • Cite real, linkable sources for any industry claim
  • Let the reader decide what fits their business

Don't

  • Assume a prospect's size, budget or competitors
  • Invent clients, results, statistics or testimonials
  • Call illustrative examples "case studies"
  • Use jargon or phrases like "big IT project"
  • Use "quietly" or "one process at a time" as headline copy
